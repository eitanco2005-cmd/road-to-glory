Jewish wedding planning checklist

Updated 2026-11-17

A Jewish wedding has its own to-do list on top of the usual venue, food and music. Here is what to plan, and when, from choosing an officiant and a date to the ketubah, the chuppah and the hora.

Every Jewish wedding blends two things: a general wedding plan (venue, catering, music, photos) and a set of Jewish rituals that need their own preparation. This checklist focuses on the second, since that is where most planning guides fall short. Practice varies widely between Orthodox, Conservative, Reform, Reconstructionist and interfaith weddings, and between Ashkenazi and Sephardic families, so use it as a starting point for a conversation with your officiant.

12+ months out: officiant and date

  • Choose your officiant first. A rabbi or cantor will shape almost every Jewish decision that follows: which rituals you include, who can serve as witnesses, ketubah text, and whether they officiate at interfaith weddings. Ask about premarital meetings or classes they require.
  • Pick a date with your officiant. Traditionally, Jewish weddings are not held on Shabbat or major festivals (Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, Sukkot, Passover, Shavuot), and many communities also avoid most of the Omer period between Passover and Shavuot (with exceptions such as Lag BaOmer) and the Three Weeks before Tisha B’Av. Customs on these periods differ between Ashkenazi and Sephardic communities, and the Reform movement does not uphold most of these restrictions. See Chabad.org’s wedding-date notes and Exploring Judaism, then confirm with your rabbi.
  • Saturday-night weddings are possible but start after Shabbat ends, which can be late in summer. Many couples choose Sunday or a Saturday night in winter.
  • Book venue and kosher caterer if needed. Check that the venue allows a chuppah, glass breaking, and a private room for yichud.

9–6 months out: ketubah, chuppah and honorees

Ketubah

The ketubah is the Jewish marriage contract, traditionally written in Aramaic and outlining obligations to the bride. Today couples choose from traditional Orthodox texts, Conservative texts (often with the Lieberman clause), and egalitarian or interfaith texts in Hebrew and English. Artists often need weeks to months for a custom piece, so order early, and have your officiant approve the text before it is printed or filled in.

Witnesses

The ketubah is signed by two witnesses. Requirements differ significantly: in Orthodox practice, witnesses are typically two observant Jewish men who are not related to the couple or to each other. Many Conservative, Reform and Reconstructionist rabbis accept witnesses of any gender, and some allow non-Jewish witnesses for certain documents or interfaith weddings (Ketubah.com). Ask your officiant before you invite anyone to sign.

Chuppah

The wedding canopy symbolizes the couple’s new home. Options include a venue-provided chuppah, a florist-built frame, or a handheld tallit held by four honored friends or relatives. Decide who will hold the poles if it is handheld.

Honorees (kibbudim)

Make a list of honors you can offer family and friends: ketubah witnesses, chuppah holders, readers of the sheva brachot (seven blessings), the person who brings the rings or reads a translation. Tracking who has been asked keeps feelings from getting hurt.

Ceremony rituals to plan

Not every couple includes all of these; My Jewish Learning, ReformJudaism.org and The Knot explain each in more depth.

  • Tisch (“table”): a lively pre-ceremony gathering, traditionally for the groom, with songs and words of Torah, often where the ketubah is signed. Many couples now hold a tisch for each partner or a joint one.
  • Ketubah signing: with the officiant and witnesses, before the ceremony.
  • Bedeken: the groom veils the bride, an Ashkenazi custom; Sephardic weddings often skip it. Egalitarian couples sometimes adapt it.
  • Processional to the chuppah: often both partners are walked in by both parents.
  • Circling: traditionally the bride circles the groom seven times (some do three); many couples now circle each other.
  • Betrothal (erusin): blessings over wine and the giving of a ring, traditionally a plain band without stones.
  • Reading the ketubah, often in Aramaic and English.
  • Sheva brachot: seven blessings over a second cup of wine, often recited by honorees.
  • Breaking the glass: a glass wrapped in cloth is stepped on, and everyone shouts “Mazel tov!” It is often explained as remembering the destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem, even in moments of joy.
  • Yichud: a few minutes of seclusion right after the ceremony. Ask your venue for a private room and arrange for food to be waiting — some couples fast on their wedding day until this moment.

3–1 months out: the reception and paperwork

  • Civil marriage license. A Jewish wedding does not replace the civil license. Rules, waiting periods and expiry dates vary by state and county, so check your county clerk’s website and make sure your officiant is authorized to sign in that state.
  • Hora and dancing. Tell your band or DJ when to start the hora (often right after you enter the reception) and whether you want to be lifted on chairs. Choose sturdy chairs and strong lifters.
  • Hamotzi and blessings. Decide who will say the blessing over the challah and, if you are including it, who will lead Birkat Hamazon (Grace after Meals) with sheva brachot at the end of the meal.
  • Kippot and programs. A short program explaining the rituals is a kind gesture for guests who have never been to a Jewish wedding.
  • Glass, wine, cups and ring: assign one person to bring them to the chuppah.

After the wedding: sheva brachot

In many traditional communities, friends and family host festive meals during the seven days after the wedding, each ending with the seven blessings (requiring a minyan and, in Orthodox practice, a new guest at each meal). If your community does this, ask a friend to coordinate hosts and dates. Then turn to thank-you notes and filing your marriage certificate.

Questions

What dates can't Jews get married on?

Traditionally, weddings are not held on Shabbat or major holidays, and many communities also avoid parts of the Omer period and the Three Weeks before Tisha B'Av. Customs vary and the Reform movement does not uphold most restrictions, so check with your rabbi.

Who can be a witness on a ketubah?

In Orthodox practice, witnesses are usually two observant Jewish men unrelated to the couple or each other. Liberal movements often accept witnesses of any gender, and requirements vary by officiant.

Is a ketubah legally binding in the US?

No. You still need a civil marriage license from your state or county, and your officiant must be authorized to sign it. Requirements vary by state.

What is yichud?

Yichud is a short period of seclusion for the couple right after the ceremony. Many couples use it to rest and eat, especially if they fasted on the wedding day.
