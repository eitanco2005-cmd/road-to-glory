Why Jews give in multiples of 18 (chai)

Updated 2026-11-17

If you have ever wondered why a bar mitzvah check says $36 or $54 instead of $50, the answer is a two-letter Hebrew word: chai, meaning life. Here is where the custom comes from, the amounts people use most, and how to handle the etiquette.

What “chai” means and where the 18 comes from

The Hebrew word chai (חי) means “alive” or “life.” It is spelled with two letters: chet (ח) and yud (י). In gematria, the traditional system that gives each Hebrew letter a numerical value, chet equals 8 and yud equals 10. Add them together and you get 18.

Because of that, 18 became a shorthand for life itself. Giving a gift or a donation in a multiple of 18 is a quiet way of saying “I wish you life” — long life, a good life, a life of blessing. As ReformJudaism.org explains, it is a custom that grew up among Jews over time; it is not a commandment, and nobody is obligated to follow it.

You will see chai everywhere once you start looking: on pendants and necklaces, on challah covers, in the toast l’chaim (“to life”), and on donation forms from Jewish charities that suggest $18, $36 or $54 instead of $25, $50 or $100. Aish and My Jewish Learning both describe chai as one of the best-known number symbols in Jewish life.

How the custom is used for gifts and tzedakah

The two places Americans meet the multiples-of-18 custom most often are life-cycle gifts and charity (tzedakah).

  • Bar and bat mitzvah gifts. A check or cash gift for $36, $54, $108 or $180 is so common that many families expect it. Many kids put part of their gift money toward savings, Israel trips or a tzedakah project.
  • Weddings. Wedding checks often follow the same pattern, typically at higher multiples.
  • Baby namings and brit milah. A modest chai gift, sometimes as a contribution to a college fund, is a familiar gesture.
  • Donations in someone’s honor or memory. Synagogues and Jewish organizations frequently list giving levels in chai multiples, and many people send $18 or $36 “in honor of” a friend’s simcha or in memory of a loved one.
  • Everyday tzedakah. Some families drop coins into a tzedakah box before Shabbat and round donations to the nearest 18 out of habit.

The number is symbolic, so the multiple you choose carries no special meaning beyond “life.” $54 is not “more blessed” than $36 — it is simply a larger gift.

Quick math: to find the nearest chai amount, divide the figure you had in mind by 18 and round. If you planned to give about $100, 100 ÷ 18 ≈ 5.6, so $90 (5 × chai) or $108 (6 × chai) are the natural choices.

How much should you give?

There is no official chart, and anyone who tells you there is a “correct” number is guessing. Advice columns such as Your Teen and JewishBoston land in a similar place: think about how close you are to the family, what you would spend on a comparable birthday or celebration gift, and what you can comfortably afford, then round to a multiple of 18.

A few factors usually shape the amount:

  • Your relationship. A classmate’s gift and a grandparent’s gift are in different categories, and everyone understands that.
  • Who is invited. If your whole family is attending, many people give more than they would as a single guest.
  • Where you live. Norms differ from community to community and region to region. If you are new to a community, a friend who has attended a few local celebrations is your best guide.
  • Your budget. A heartfelt card with $18 is a perfectly respectable gift. Generosity is measured against what you can give, not against the event’s price tag.
You will find websites that publish precise “going rates.” Many are not based on any survey. Treat any specific figure as one person’s opinion, and don’t try to “cover your plate” — gifts are not meant as reimbursement for the party.

Etiquette tips

  • Checks are normal. A check in an envelope with a card is the classic bar/bat mitzvah gift. Make it out to the child (or as the family requests); some families prefer gifts to a 529 plan or a named charity.
  • Don’t bring gifts on Shabbat if the family observes it. In many communities, guests don’t carry or exchange money on Shabbat. If the service is Saturday morning, send the gift beforehand, mail it afterward, or bring it to a Saturday-night or Sunday party. Ask the family or the synagogue if you are unsure.
  • Non-Jewish guests are welcome to join in. Giving in multiples of 18 is a kind gesture from anyone, and families appreciate it. It is equally fine to give a non-chai amount.
  • Non-cash gifts are fine too. Books, Judaica such as a kiddush cup or tallit bag, or an experience gift are all appropriate. Some people add $18 in a card to a physical gift for the symbolism.
  • Write a note. A few warm sentences about the child or couple often means more than the amount.

A little more background

Gematria has long been used in Jewish study to draw connections between words that share a numerical value, and the 18–life association is one of the most popular examples. Chabad.org discusses other appearances of the number in Jewish texts and notes that some people see a precedent for giving in units of 18. Customs like this one are part of the texture of Jewish life rather than law, which is why practice varies so much between families and communities. If you want to know how your community approaches gifts or donations, your rabbi or synagogue office is always a good person to ask.

Questions

Why is 18 a lucky number in Judaism?

The Hebrew word chai, meaning life, is spelled chet (8) and yud (10), which add up to 18 in gematria. Giving in multiples of 18 symbolically wishes the recipient life; it is a custom, not a religious requirement.

Is $36 an appropriate bar mitzvah gift?

Yes. $36, or double chai, is one of the most common gifts, especially from classmates and casual acquaintances. The right amount depends on your relationship and budget, and norms vary by community.

Do I have to give in multiples of 18?

No. It is a widely loved custom, but any sincere gift is appropriate, and non-Jewish guests are not expected to know about it.

Can I bring a check to a Saturday morning bar mitzvah?

In many observant communities money is not handled on Shabbat, so it is better to mail the gift or bring it to an evening or Sunday party. If you are unsure, ask the family.
