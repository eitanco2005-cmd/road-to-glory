Hosting Rosh Hashanah dinner: a practical guide

Updated 2026-11-17

Rosh Hashanah dinner is about sweetness, family and a fresh start. Here is how the meal flows, what the symbolic foods mean, and a timeline that lets you cook ahead and enjoy your own table.

When is Rosh Hashanah 2027, and what happens at dinner?

Rosh Hashanah 5788 begins at sundown on Friday, October 1, 2027, and ends at nightfall on Sunday, October 3 (Hebcal). Because the first night is also Shabbat, many families include Shabbat additions to candle-lighting and kiddush, and some customs (such as shofar blowing) shift to the second day. Your synagogue’s calendar or your rabbi can tell you what your community does.

The evening meal follows a familiar order, explained well by My Jewish Learning:

  1. Candle-lighting before sundown (or from an existing flame on the second night, per traditional practice).
  2. Kiddush over wine or grape juice, with the shehecheyanu blessing.
  3. Hand-washing (in traditional homes) and hamotzi over a round challah, often dipped in honey instead of salt.
  4. Apples and honey, with the prayer for “a good and sweet year.”
  5. Simanim (symbolic foods), if your family includes them.
  6. The meal, followed by Birkat Hamazon (Grace after Meals) in many homes.

The simanim: symbolic foods

Many families, especially Sephardic and Mizrahi ones, hold a short “Rosh Hashanah seder” of symbolic foods before the meal. Each is eaten with a short yehi ratzon (“may it be Your will”) wish, often based on wordplay in Hebrew, Aramaic or Yiddish. You don’t need all of them; pick a few that fit your table. ReformJudaism.org and PJ Library have kid-friendly explanations.

On the second night, many families eat a “new fruit” they haven’t tasted this season — often a pomegranate, starfruit or dragon fruit — and say shehecheyanu over it. Some Ashkenazi families also avoid nuts on Rosh Hashanah; ask relatives about your own family’s customs.

Round challah: the round loaf is usually explained as the cycle of the year, or a crown for God’s sovereignty. Many bakeries sell round raisin challahs before the holiday — order ahead, as they sell out.

Building the menu

The theme is sweetness. Classic Ashkenazi-American menus lean on brisket and honey cake; Sephardic tables might feature fish, stuffed vegetables and leek patties. A balanced menu might include:

  • To start: round challah, apples and honey, simanim plate
  • First course: chicken soup with matzah balls or kreplach, or a fish dish (gefilte fish, Moroccan-style spicy fish)
  • Main: brisket, roast chicken with honey and apples, or a vegetarian main such as stuffed squash
  • Sides: tzimmes (sweet carrots), noodle kugel, roasted root vegetables, green beans or a leek dish
  • Dessert: honey cake, apple cake, or a fruit platter with pomegranate

If anyone keeps kosher, plan the menu as either meat or dairy (most holiday meals are meat), use pareve desserts, and ask whether they need disposable dishes. If you are hosting both nights, plan two different menus or turn night one’s leftovers into night two’s sides.

Hosting timeline and make-ahead plan

3–4 weeks before

  • Invite guests and ask about dietary needs. Check whether guests have services that evening and set your start time accordingly.
  • Plan the menu and decide which dishes guests can bring. For more step-by-step ideas, see Kveller’s 14-step plan and Hey Alma’s first-timer guide.

1–2 weeks before

  • Order challah. Buy wine, honey, apples and non-perishables.
  • Cook and freeze: soup, matzah balls or kreplach, brisket (it slices better after chilling), honey cake.

2–3 days before

  • Shop for produce. Make kugel and tzimmes (both reheat well). Set the table.

Day of

  • Thaw and reheat, roast vegetables, plate the simanim. Lay out candles, kiddush cup, challah cover, honey dish.
  • If your home observes the holiday, finish cooking before candle-lighting; traditional practice limits cooking on the holiday itself, and the rules are stricter when it falls on Shabbat, as in 2027. Ask your rabbi about specifics.

How much food to make

There is no authoritative portion standard for a holiday meal, and appetites vary. Rather than relying on a formula, count guests (adults and kids separately), decide how many courses you will serve, and check the yields in your recipes. Most hosts aim for leftovers rather than running short — especially with two nights of meals. If you buy prepared food, caterers and kosher markets usually list how many people each tray serves; follow their guidance.

Table and guest tips

  • Explain as you go. If some guests are new to Rosh Hashanah, print a one-page card with the blessings in transliteration and a line about each siman. People love being handed a role, such as reading a wish or passing the honey.
  • Set a sweet table. A small honey dish at each end, a bowl of apples and a pomegranate make a simple centerpiece.
  • Include the kids. Let them dip the apples, braid or decorate the challah, or write their own wishes for the year.
  • Make room for reflection. Rosh Hashanah opens the Ten Days of Repentance. Some hosts invite everyone to share a hope for the coming year between courses.
  • Plan for the next day. If guests are going to services in the morning, keep dessert simple and the evening relaxed.
Customs around Rosh Hashanah meals — which simanim to eat, when to light candles on the second night, whether to cook on the holiday — vary between movements and families. When in doubt, ask your rabbi.

Questions

When is Rosh Hashanah 2027?

Rosh Hashanah 5788 begins at sundown on Friday, October 1, 2027, and ends at nightfall on Sunday, October 3. The first night is also Shabbat.

What are simanim?

Simanim are symbolic foods eaten at the start of the Rosh Hashanah meal, each paired with a short wish for the new year, often based on wordplay. Common ones include apples and honey, pomegranate, dates, leeks, beets and a fish head.

Why is challah round on Rosh Hashanah?

The round shape is usually explained as the cycle of the year or as a crown symbolizing God's sovereignty. It is often dipped in honey instead of salt for a sweet year.

What should I serve at Rosh Hashanah dinner?

Many families serve round challah, apples and honey, chicken soup, brisket or roast chicken, tzimmes, kugel and honey cake. Sephardic menus often feature fish, leeks and stuffed vegetables.
