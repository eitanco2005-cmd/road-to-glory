Bar and bat mitzvah planning timeline, month by month

Updated 2026-11-17

A bar or bat mitzvah has two tracks running side by side: your child's learning and the celebration. This timeline lays out both, from the date your synagogue assigns to the last thank-you note.

Every synagogue runs its own b’nai mitzvah program, so treat this as a framework and slot in the dates your clergy and education director give you. Families often start thinking about the celebration 12–18 months out, and some congregations begin the process even earlier; My Jewish Learning and planning checklists such as The Bash suggest a similar sequence.

18–12 months before: the date and the big decisions

  • Get your date from the synagogue. Most congregations assign or confirm dates themselves, usually based on the child’s Hebrew birthday (13 for boys; 12 or 13 for girls, depending on the community) and the calendar of Torah portions. Some assign dates two or more years ahead. Don’t book anything until the date is confirmed in writing.
  • Learn the program requirements. Ask about required classes, attendance at services, tutoring arrangements, fees, and what is expected of the family (for example, sponsoring the kiddush or oneg).
  • Decide on the shape of the celebration. Kiddush luncheon after services, Saturday-night party, Sunday brunch, a family trip — or a mix. This one decision drives most of your budget and vendor list.
  • Set a budget and a draft guest list. Even a rough headcount (adults vs. kids) makes venue and caterer conversations much easier.
  • Book the venue and caterer if you are holding an evening party. Popular venues, photographers and DJs in busy areas can book a year or more ahead.

12–6 months before: learning, mitzvah project and vendors

  • Start tutoring. Many programs begin formal tutoring about a year out, often weekly. Your child will typically prepare to chant from the Torah and/or Haftarah, lead some prayers, and write a short speech (a d’var Torah) about the portion.
  • Choose a mitzvah project. Many congregations ask for a service or tzedakah project. The best ones grow out of something the child already cares about — animals, sports, food insecurity, a family story.
  • Book remaining vendors: photographer, DJ or band, florist/decor, invitations designer, kippot or favors.
  • Block hotel rooms for out-of-town guests, ideally within walking distance of the synagogue if guests will walk on Shabbat.
  • Send save-the-dates around 6–9 months ahead if many guests travel.
  • Buy or choose ritual items: tallit, tefillin (in communities where they are customary), kippah, and anything else your synagogue expects.

6–2 months before: honors, details and invitations

  • Plan the honors. Ask your synagogue how many aliyot (Torah blessings) and other honors are available to the family — opening the ark, holding or dressing the Torah, reading a prayer — and who is eligible. Rules for non-Jewish relatives and for who may have an aliyah vary from congregation to congregation, so ask rather than assume.
  • Plan the candle-lighting if you are having one at the party: typically around 13 candles, each honoring a relative or group of friends, often with a short rhyme or a song. Keep the list and the order in one place so you can rebalance it as RSVPs come in.
  • Finalize the invitation wording and order printing (or set up digital invitations).
  • Mail invitations about 6–8 weeks ahead — a common etiquette guideline — and earlier (two to three months) for guests who must travel. Set the RSVP deadline about three to four weeks before the event so you can give the caterer a count.
  • Choose the outfit(s) and schedule a portrait or photo session if you want one.
  • Check in with the tutor and rabbi on your child’s progress and the d’var Torah draft.

The last month

  • Chase missing RSVPs and give the caterer a final count by the deadline in your contract.
  • Make a seating chart, a kids’ table plan, and a vendor contact sheet with arrival times.
  • Confirm honors with each honoree, including the Hebrew names the rabbi will need for aliyot.
  • Rehearse in the sanctuary. Many synagogues schedule a run-through with the rabbi or cantor in the final week or two.
  • Prepare the payment envelopes and tips for vendors.
  • Pack a day-of kit: speech copies, tallit, safety pins, snacks, phone charger, a pen for the guest book.
Shabbat note: If your family or synagogue observes Shabbat, photography, phones and money may not be permitted on Saturday. Plan the photo session for another day, and let guests know whether gifts should be mailed or brought to an evening party.

After the celebration

  • Log every gift as soon as you open it — name, amount or item, and address. It is far harder to reconstruct later.
  • Thank-you notes. Handwritten notes from the bar or bat mitzvah are still the norm. Many etiquette guides suggest aiming to send them within a few weeks and not letting them drift past a couple of months. Mention the specific gift and, where possible, something personal.
  • Follow through on tzedakah. Many families encourage the child to donate part of their gift money, often in multiples of 18 (why 18?).
  • Pay final vendor balances and send reviews or thank-yous to the tutor and synagogue staff.

Timelines from different sources disagree by a few months here and there — for example, New York Family suggests booking further ahead in competitive markets. The order of tasks matters more than the exact month. And whenever your synagogue’s schedule differs from any generic checklist, including this one, follow the synagogue.

Questions

How far in advance should you plan a bar mitzvah?

Many families start 12 to 18 months ahead, and synagogues often assign dates even earlier. Popular venues and vendors in busy areas can book a year or more out.

When should bar mitzvah invitations go out?

A common guideline is about 6 to 8 weeks before the event, with save-the-dates months earlier and earlier invitations for out-of-town guests. Set the RSVP deadline a few weeks ahead of the caterer's deadline.

When does bar mitzvah tutoring start?

Many programs start formal tutoring about a year before the date, but this varies by synagogue and by how much the child will chant or lead. Ask your education director for your congregation's schedule.

Who gets an aliyah at a bar mitzvah?

Usually close family members are honored, but the number of aliyot and who is eligible depend on the synagogue and movement. Your rabbi or b'nai mitzvah coordinator will explain the rules.
