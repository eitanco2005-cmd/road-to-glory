How to make a wedding seating chart, step by step

Updated 2026-11-18

Seating feels like a puzzle with 150 pieces. It gets much easier when you do it in the right order: the room first, then groups, then the tricky people, then the details.

When to start: sketch the room as soon as you have a venue, but do the real chart after most RSVPs are in. Guides differ a little on timing: Paperlust suggests a first draft about 5–6 weeks out, while others wait until the RSVP deadline, 3–4 weeks before the date. Either way, leave a week before the caterer’s final-count deadline.

1. Get the floor plan and real table sizes

Ask the venue for a floor plan with the dance floor, bars, buffet, exits and speakers marked, and for the exact table sizes. A 60-inch round is usually listed at 8–10 guests and a 72-inch round at 10–12 (Party Rental Ltd.). Ask what they recommend: chargers, chair widths and centerpieces change the answer. See our round-table guide for the arithmetic.

2. Turn your RSVPs into parties

Make one row per invitation with the number of confirmed seats, the side (yours, theirs, both) and a specific group label. “Mom’s book club” beats “friends”. If you keep a guest list in this format, the next steps take minutes.

3. Place the couple first

Decide between a sweetheart table (just the two of you, both families feel equal) and a head table (you plus the wedding party, often with their partners). Put it where everyone can see you and close to the dance floor.

4. Seat the families closest to you

Parents, grandparents and siblings get the tables nearest the couple. Many couples give each set of parents their own table so they can host their own relatives and friends. If parents are divorced, two tables at the same distance from you avoid any sense of rank.

5. Fill tables group by group

Now the bulk of the work: each group becomes a table. A group of 10 is a table. A group of 14 becomes one table of 8 and one of 6 that you top up with a related group (college friends + their partners, work friends + the plus-ones). Split big groups along lines that already exist, like class year or department, rather than at random.

Fitting groups to tables
Group sizeWhat to do
Fits one table exactlySeat it as is
A few too manyMove the couple with the loosest tie to the group to a neighboring, related table
A few too fewFill with guests who know at least one person there
Twice a table or moreSplit along existing lines; place the two tables side by side

6. Build mixer tables from the leftovers

Every wedding has guests who don’t belong to any group: a neighbor, a mentor, a far-flung cousin. Put them together at a mixer table and make sure each has at least one person they know. Seat your most outgoing friends there as hosts.

7. Solve conflicts and special needs

  • Keep-apart pairs: exes, feuding relatives. Different tables, and not in each other’s line of sight if you can help it.
  • Older guests: near an aisle and the restrooms, away from the speakers. This is one of the most common seating complaints (The Knot).
  • Accessibility: a wheelchair needs an open spot at the table, not a chair squeezed out.
  • Kids: young children usually sit with their parents; a kids’ table works for older children with a supervising adult nearby (Bridebook).
  • Singles: don’t build a “singles table”; seat people with friends.

8. Check the numbers

  • Every confirmed guest is seated.
  • No table is over capacity (count high chairs).
  • Meal choices per table are totaled for the caterer.
  • There is one spare table or a few spare seats for surprises.

9. Make the escort list and send everything out

Print an alphabetical list (last name, then table) for escort cards or the display board. Send the table plan with table numbers and meal counts to the venue and caterer, and keep a copy for the day-of coordinator.

Questions

How far in advance should you do a wedding seating chart?

Draft it when most RSVPs are in, about 3 to 6 weeks before the wedding depending on your RSVP deadline, and finalize it at least a week before the caterer needs final numbers.

Who sits closest to the couple?

Parents, grandparents and siblings, then close family and the wedding party's partners. Divorced parents can each host a table at the same distance from the couple.

Should couples be split up at the wedding?

No. Keep couples and families together; split groups of friends instead, along natural lines like class year or team.

What is the fastest way to make a seating chart?

Group guests by a specific label first, then turn each group into a table. A seating tool with auto-seat and drag-and-drop does most of the arithmetic for you.
