Seating chart for round tables: capacity, table count and layout

Updated 2026-11-18

Round tables are the default at weddings and b'nai mitzvah because everyone can talk to everyone. The catch is the math: how many people actually fit, and how many tables you need.

How many people fit at a round table?

Published capacity charts agree on the range, and differ on how tight is acceptable. These are the figures rental companies and planners typically use:

Chiavari chairs are narrower than banquet chairs, so venues using them often fit the higher number; big centerpieces and chargers push you toward the lower one. Always ask the venue what they set.

How many round tables do you need?

Tables = guests ÷ comfortable seats per table, rounded up, plus one spare. The spare absorbs late RSVPs and groups that don’t divide neatly. Don’t count the head or sweetheart table in the division.

Notice the trade-off: 72-inch tables at 12 save tables (and centerpieces, linens and floor space), but conversation across a 6-foot table is harder. Many couples mix sizes: 72-inch rounds for big family tables, 60-inch for friend groups.

How much room do round tables take?

A seated guest needs roughly 18–24 inches behind the table edge for the chair and to stand up, and servers need an aisle on top of that. A practical planning figure is a square of about 10–12 feet per 60-inch table and 11–13 feet per 72-inch table, including chairs and walkways. That is a rule of thumb only: the venue’s floor plan and fire-code capacity are what count, along with the dance floor, the buffet or bar lines and any accessible routes.

Seating round tables without splitting families

Round tables punish odd numbers. A family of 5 and a couple make 7, which leaves one awkward seat at a table of 8. Some fixes that work:

  • Seat parties, not people. Work in invitations (a couple, a family of four) and fill tables with whole parties. Never split a couple to make the numbers work.
  • Allow 9 or 11. A 60-inch round set for 9 or a 72-inch set for 11 looks normal and solves most leftovers. Tell the venue the per-table count in advance.
  • Use the spare table for overflow. Three couples who know each other loosely make a better table than six seats scattered across the room.
  • Keep the empty seat away from the dance floor. If a table has one empty seat, put it where it faces the couple, so nobody sits with their back to the action.
  • Kids’ tables can be smaller. A 48-inch round with 6 kids plus booster seats is easier than squeezing 10 children around a 60-inch table.

Example: 130 guests on 60-inch rounds

130 guests ÷ 10 = 13 tables, plus one spare = 14 rounds, and a sweetheart table. In practice, groups won’t divide into exact tens, so a typical result looks like: 9 tables of 10, 3 tables of 9, 1 table of 8 and a spare with 5 late RSVPs. That’s still 14 rounds, and nobody is split from their partner.

Round tables vs. long tables

Long (banquet or farm) tables seat more people in the same floor area and look great for a family-style dinner, but you can only talk to the three or four people near you. Rounds are more social and easier to assign by group. Many venues mix both: a long head table and rounds for everyone else. Questions

How many people can sit at a 60-inch round table?

Usually 8 comfortably and up to 10. Rental companies list it at 8-10; venues using slim Chiavari chairs often seat 10.

How many people fit at a 72-inch round table?

Usually 10 comfortably and up to 12.

How many round tables do I need for 150 guests?

At 10 per 60-inch table, 15 tables plus one spare. At 8 per table, 19 plus a spare. At 12 per 72-inch table, 13 plus a spare.

Is it OK to have 9 people at a table?

Yes. Odd counts like 9 or 11 are common and are better than splitting a couple or family. Tell the venue the count per table so place settings match.
