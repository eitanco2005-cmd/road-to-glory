Bar and bat mitzvah seating chart: how to seat kids, teens and adults

Updated 2026-11-18

A bar or bat mitzvah party seats two very different crowds: a pack of 13-year-olds who will barely sit down, and adults who came to talk. A good chart plans for both.

The service itself usually needs no chart: synagogue seating is open, and a kiddush lunch after services is often buffet-style. The seating chart is for the party, whether that’s Saturday night, Sunday brunch or a luncheon. Here’s how to divide the room.

1. The mitzvah kids’ section

The b’nai mitzvah’s friends are the heart of the party, and they spend most of it on the dance floor. Common set-ups (Prismm):

  • A long, U- or E-shaped table near the dance floor, where the guest of honor sits with friends.
  • A kids’ lounge with open seating: high tables, stools or couches. Many parties skip assigned seats for this group entirely.
  • Several round tables grouped together, with friend groups (school, camp, team) at separate tables.

Whichever you choose, keep it close to the DJ and away from the grandparents. Budget kid-friendly food and a kids’ meal count separately for the caterer.

2. Younger children

Children under about 8 usually sit with their parents. If you have many young cousins, a supervised kids’ table with activity packs works, but put it near the parents’ tables, not across the room (Pleasantdale Chateau).

3. Older teens and young adults

High-schoolers and college-age cousins rarely want to sit with 13-year-olds, or with their parents. Give them their own table between the kids’ section and the dance floor. If most of them are cousins, a combined cousins’ table often works best.

4. Family and honorees

Seat grandparents and the immediate family nearest the front, with a clear path to the dance floor and the candle-lighting spot. If you’re holding a candle-lighting ceremony (a party custom, typically about 13 candles honoring relatives and friends; see Ritualwell), seat the honorees close enough to come up quickly when called. Keep a list of honorees next to the seating chart so you can check every one of them has a seat near the front.

5. Adults’ tables

The rest is like a wedding: one table per group (synagogue friends, work, neighbors, parents of the mitzvah kids who stay), seated by how well they know each other rather than by importance. Put adults who want to talk furthest from the speakers. For table sizes and counts, see round tables.

Seating chart checklist

  • Kids’ RSVPs confirmed separately (they change late).
  • Kids’ meal count and any allergies sent to the caterer.
  • Every candle-lighting honoree has a seat near the front.
  • Grandparents near an aisle and away from the speakers.
  • Divorced or separated family members at tables of equal standing.
  • A chaperone or two seated with or next to the kids’ section.
  • Escort cards sorted A–Z by last name; kids’ cards in their own section.
  • Table plan and counts sent to the venue and the DJ (for the candle-lighting order).
Shabbat note: if the party is on Shabbat or the event follows Shabbat customs, prepare everything printed (escort cards, the honorees list) before Shabbat begins, and ask the venue to set them out.

Questions

Do the bar mitzvah kids need assigned seats?

Often not. Many parties give the mitzvah kids a long table or lounge with open seating near the dance floor, since they spend most of the party dancing. Assign seats if you need to manage friend groups or allergies.

Where should young children sit at a bar mitzvah?

Children under about 8 usually sit with their parents. A supervised kids' table near the parents can work if there are many young cousins.

Where do candle-lighting honorees sit?

Near the front, close to where the candles are lit, so they can come up quickly when the DJ or emcee calls them.

How many kids' tables do I need?

Count kids separately from adults: one long table for the mitzvah kids or rounds of 8-10, plus a table for teens and young adults if you have more than a handful.
